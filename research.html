Research: Toward a predictive science of deep learning

My goal is to uncover the principles governing deep learning and use them to explain mechanisms, make quantitative predictions, and inform algorithm design. I pursue this goal through three complementary perspectives:

  • Physics. Finding the right level of description: deciding which details matter at a given scale and building toy models to isolate the underlying mechanisms.

  • Mathematics. Establishing rigorous results under explicit conditions and clarifying the scope of a theory.

  • Engineering. Stress-testing theory in real-world systems and refining it through discrepancies between predictions and observations.

Inspired by physics, I view scale as an organizing principle for deep learning. I do not expect a single theory to capture learning across all scales; different scales require different effective theories.

This perspective suggests two complementary routes toward understanding deep learning:

  • Bottom-up. Starting from simple models and microscopic mechanisms and working toward more complex learning systems.

  • Top-down. Starting from macroscopic laws and using them to guide the study of underlying mechanisms and intermediate-scale dynamics.

Much of my earlier work followed the bottom-up route. I have come to see a limitation of developing theory from microscopic models alone: without constraints from macroscopic observations, it is difficult to determine which modeling choices and mechanisms are relevant to real-world training. I am now exploring the top-down route, using observed regularities to guide toy model construction and analysis, with the hope that the two approaches will eventually inform one another.

Current focus: LLM pretraining

Large language model (LLM) pretraining optimizes a next-token prediction objective at an enormous scale:

\[ \min_{\theta} \mathcal{R}(\theta),\qquad \mathcal{R}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{D}} \left[-\sum_{k=2}^{L}\log P_{\theta}(x_k\mid x_1,\dots,x_{k-1})\right]. \]

Here, \(P_\theta\) is a Transformer language model, and \(\mathcal{D}\) is an internet-scale text corpus. Despite its complex training dynamics, pretraining exhibits simple macroscopic regularities, such as scaling laws. My research seeks to explain these regularities and use them to guide training through three connected levels:

  • Macroscopic laws. Identify collective variables that capture pretraining and quantitative laws relating them across scales.

  • Microscopic mechanisms. Understand how these laws emerge from high-dimensional learning dynamics.

  • Training principles. Guide optimizer and architecture design and hyperparameter selection as training scales up.

For an overview, see my talk slides. Selected work includes:

  • Functional scaling law (FSL). Modeling loss dynamics as a functional of hyperparameter schedules: NeurIPS 2025, ICLR 2026, COLT 2026.

  • ELR collapse. Pretraining dynamics are governed primarily by the effective learning rate (ELR), rather than the nominal learning rate alone: arXiv 2026.

  • Optimization mechanisms. Understanding how optimizer design shapes training dynamics: arXiv 2026, ICML 2025.

Earlier work

My earlier work developed mathematical perspectives on neural-network function spaces and SGD dynamics. These studies helped shape my current approach to understanding large-scale learning.

  • Dynamical stability of SGD. Why does SGD favor some solutions over others that fit the data equally well? We studied this question through dynamical stability and the geometry of SGD noise, shedding light on implicit regularization in overparameterized learning.

    • Talk slides

    • Selected papers: NeurIPS 2018, NeurIPS 2022, ICML 2023, and TMLR 2026.

  • Function-space theory of neural networks. When and why can neural networks efficiently approximate and learn functions in high dimensions, and how do these capabilities differ from those of kernel and random-feature methods? We studied these questions through the function spaces associated with neural networks, connecting approximation properties, architectural inductive biases, and generalization.

    • Talk slides

    • Selected papers: Constructive Approximation 2021, JMLR 2022, NeurIPS 2023, and AoS 2025.
